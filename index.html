How to actually improve

The drills are the easy part. This is what makes them work.

Pause instead of “um”

A filler is what a mouth does while a brain catches up. Silence does the same job and sounds deliberate. When you feel an “um” coming, close your mouth. Two seconds of quiet reads as confidence; two seconds of “uhh” reads as panic.

Decide your ending first

Rambling is what happens when you start a sentence without knowing where it lands. Before you open your mouth, pick the last sentence. Everything before it is just the route there.

Speak in shorter sentences

Long sentences need working memory you don’t have while nervous. Full stops are free. They give you legal, invisible places to breathe and think.

Listen to the playback

Every session records locally so you can hear yourself. It’s uncomfortable for about a week, then it becomes the fastest feedback loop you have. Confidence is mostly familiarity with your own voice.

What the numbers mean

Every score is 0–100. None of them are the point on their own.

Pace

Words per minute across the whole session. 138–165 is the band broadcasters aim for. Faster and the room stops processing; slower and you lose them. Also reported is your rate while actually speaking, which strips the pauses out.

Fillers

Counted per hundred words, so a long fluent answer isn’t punished for the same slip a short one makes. Hard fillers — um, uh, er — weigh double. Crutch words like basically and you know only count when you chose them, never when they’re in a passage you were told to read.

Projection

How loud you are in decibels, measured only while you’re actually speaking. Both mumbling and clipping cost you. Read it next to voice above room: a voice six decibels over the background isn’t carrying, however loud it feels from the inside.

Diction & passage read

On the reading drills these are deliberately separate. Diction is how many of the words you reached came through; passage read is how far you got. Stopping early is a pacing problem, not a pronunciation one, and mixing them makes a careful reader look unintelligible.
